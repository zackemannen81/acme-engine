# When Correction Competes with Repetition:
## Source Reliability and the Updating of Contradictory Episodic Details

**Synthetic benchmark manuscript — ACME-CM-001**  
**This manuscript, its authors, institution, participants, and empirical data are fictional. It is intended for software evaluation and must not be represented as a completed or published human-subjects study.**

Mira Lind, Elias Novak, and Hanna Qureshi  
Cognitive Systems Laboratory, Northbridge Institute for Mind and Behavior

### Abstract

Human remembering depends not only on whether information was encountered, but also on which source is judged to be responsible for it. We tested whether source reliability and repetition jointly determine how people update episodic memories after receiving information that contradicts an observed event. In a preregistered experiment, 192 adults viewed a short film, later read a misleading event report either once or three times, and then received a correction attributed to either a forensic analyst or a graduate assistant. The final analysis included 184 participants. High-reliability corrections reduced endorsement of misinformation, improved source attribution, and eliminated the adverse effect of repeated misinformation. These findings suggest that correction succeeds when a credible source provides a sufficiently diagnostic replacement for the outdated detail. They also indicate that contradiction resolution is better characterized as source-sensitive memory updating than as simple deletion of the original representation.

**Keywords:** episodic memory, misinformation, source monitoring, correction, contradiction, memory updating

## 1. Introduction

Episodic memory is constructive rather than a literal recording of experience. Remembered details are assembled from perceptual traces, semantic expectations, later information, and judgments about where each detail originated. The source-monitoring framework proposes that people attribute mental content to sources by evaluating qualitative characteristics such as perceptual detail, contextual information, familiarity, and cognitive operations (Johnson, Hashtroudi, & Lindsay, 1993). When these characteristics overlap, a later verbal description may be mistaken for something that was directly perceived.

The misinformation effect demonstrates that misleading post-event information can alter subsequent reports of an earlier event (Loftus, 2005). Corrections reduce this influence, but corrected information can continue to affect reasoning and recall even when the correction itself is remembered (Ecker, Lewandowsky, & Tang, 2010; Lewandowsky et al., 2012). A correction therefore need not erase a false representation. Instead, accurate and inaccurate representations may remain available and compete during retrieval.

Two additional findings motivate the present study. First, retrieval can strengthen selected information while impairing related competitors, a pattern known as retrieval-induced forgetting (Anderson, Bjork, & Bjork, 1994). Repeated exposure to a misleading detail may similarly increase its accessibility relative to the original event detail. Second, reactivation can render an episodic memory susceptible to integration of new information (Hupbach, Gomez, Hardt, & Nadel, 2007). From this perspective, a correction presented after reactivation may be incorporated into an existing event representation rather than stored as an isolated fact.

The current experiment examined whether the effectiveness of correction depends on two properties of the competing representations: the retrieval advantage produced by repetition and the diagnostic value of the correcting source. Participants watched a filmed event containing several critical details. They subsequently encountered contradictory descriptions of those details either once or three times. A correction then identified the original details and was attributed either to a forensic analyst with direct access to the recording or to a graduate assistant who had read a written summary.

The preregistered primary outcome was source-attribution accuracy for corrected details. We expected three presentations of misinformation to increase false endorsement, a high-reliability correction to reduce false endorsement, and source reliability to attenuate the effect of repetition. We further expected confidence to remain imperfectly calibrated: some participants would confidently endorse a misleading detail despite correctly remembering that a correction had occurred.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Participants

A total of 192 English-speaking adults between 18 and 35 years of age were recruited from the Northbridge community participant pool. Six participants were removed under preregistered criteria: three did not complete the delayed session, two failed both embedded attention checks, and one experienced a video-playback failure. The analyzed sample therefore contained 186 participants.

Participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of a neurological condition affecting memory. The exclusion protocol also specified that left-handed participants were ineligible because the broader project included a planned lateralization analysis. Participants received the equivalent of USD 35.

### 2.2 Design

The experiment used a 2 × 2 between-participants design:

1. **Misinformation repetition:** one presentation or three presentations.
2. **Correction-source reliability:** high reliability or low reliability.

Randomization was stratified by age band and self-reported gender. Before the post-lock audit, the four cells contained 47, 47, 46, and 46 participants, respectively.

The study was preregistered before data collection. Protocol Version 1 identified source-attribution accuracy as the primary outcome. Amendment 1, timestamped before the first participant was tested, changed the primary outcome to misinformation endorsement at the delayed test because pilot data showed a ceiling effect in broad source recognition. Source attribution remained the principal secondary outcome.

### 2.3 Materials

#### Event film

Participants viewed a 6-minute film depicting a student entering a university library, returning several objects, speaking with a staff member, and leaving through a side entrance. The film contained eight critical details selected during pilot testing. One critical detail was a **red bicycle** secured outside the entrance. Because of the camera's white-balance setting, several pilot viewers informally described the bicycle as orange-red, but all preregistered coding materials classified it as red.

Two additional details appeared in the recognition test as noncritical fillers. Thus, some analysis scripts contained ten labeled rows even though only eight items contributed to the critical-item outcome.

#### Misleading report

Thirty minutes after the film, participants read a 420-word summary presented as an independent witness report. Each of the eight critical event details was replaced with a plausible contradictory detail. For example, the red bicycle was described as blue. In the single-presentation condition, each misleading sentence appeared once. In the three-presentation condition, the same claim appeared in the initial summary, a bulleted recap, and a brief comprehension prompt.

#### Correction

Ten minutes later, participants read a correction identifying the eight original event details. In the high-reliability condition, the correction was attributed to a forensic video analyst who had reviewed the original recording frame by frame. In the low-reliability condition, it was attributed to a graduate assistant who had read another participant's notes.

### 2.4 Procedure

Participants completed encoding and misinformation phases in individual testing cubicles. Cohort A, comprising participant IDs 001–096, was tested in Room 214. After a laboratory relocation, Cohort B, comprising IDs 097–192, was tested in Room 318. Room assignment was not experimentally manipulated.

After informed consent, participants viewed the film without being told that memory would be tested. They completed a 30-minute visuospatial filler task, read the misleading report, and then received the correction. Participants returned approximately 24 hours later (median = 24.3 hours, range = 22.1–27.8 hours) for the memory assessment.

The preregistration originally specified a 48-hour retention interval. A scheduling amendment made before enrollment began changed the implemented interval to approximately 24 hours. The 48-hour value therefore describes the initial plan, not the observed procedure.

At test, participants completed:

- free recall of the filmed event;
- an eight-item forced-choice test containing the original detail, the misleading detail, and two new alternatives;
- a source judgment for each selected answer;
- confidence ratings from 0 to 100;
- two filler recognition items that were excluded from the critical-item score.

### 2.5 Measures

The primary dependent variable was the proportion of corrected critical items attributed to the original film source. Misinformation endorsement was coded when a participant selected the misleading alternative, regardless of the source subsequently assigned to it. Source-attribution accuracy measured whether the selected detail was assigned to the film, witness report, correction, or was labeled as guessed.

The main analysis used a logistic mixed-effects model with random intercepts for participant and item. Fixed effects were repetition, correction-source reliability, and their interaction. Condition coding was centered at −0.5 and +0.5.

### 2.6 Data exclusions and integrity checks

Trials with response times below 250 ms were excluded. Participants were not excluded on the basis of handedness; the sentence describing handedness in Section 2.1 was retained from a pilot protocol and does not describe the implemented study. No participant was removed for failing a single attention check.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Manipulation checks

Participants rated the forensic analyst as more reliable (M = 82.6, SD = 11.4) than the graduate assistant (M = 56.8, SD = 15.7), *t*(184) = 12.91, *p* < .001. Recognition that a correction had occurred was high in both conditions (91% and 88%, respectively).

### 3.2 Misinformation endorsement

Across conditions, participants endorsed the misleading alternative on 19% of corrected critical trials. Endorsement was lower after a high-reliability correction than after a low-reliability correction, *b* = −0.71, SE = 0.21, *z* = −3.38, *p* < .001, odds ratio (OR) = 0.49. Repetition increased endorsement, *b* = 0.46, SE = 0.19, *z* = 2.42, *p* = .016, OR = 1.58. The interaction was not statistically significant, *b* = −0.29, SE = 0.24, *z* = −1.21, *p* = .226.

Observed misinformation-endorsement rates were:

| Correction source | One presentation | Three presentations |
|---|---:|---:|
| High reliability | .12 | .16 |
| Low reliability | .20 | .28 |

A planned contrast comparing high- and low-reliability corrections following repeated misinformation produced OR = 0.61, 95% CI [0.42, 0.88], *p* = .018. This contrast was reported as *p* = .081 in an earlier narrative export because the final two digits were transposed.

Although the descriptive difference between one and three misinformation presentations was smaller in the high-reliability condition, the nonsignificant interaction does not establish that the repetition effect was absent.

### 3.3 Correct-detail recall

Participants selected the original event detail on 68% of critical trials after a high-reliability correction and 59% after a low-reliability correction. Repetition reduced correct-detail selection by approximately 6 percentage points. Performance for the bicycle item followed the overall pattern: the preregistered correct category was red, the misleading alternative was blue, and orange-red descriptions were accepted only in free recall because they preserved the coded hue family.

### 3.4 Source attribution

Conditional on choosing the original detail, participants attributed it to the film on 76% of trials in the high-reliability condition and 62% in the low-reliability condition. Some participants selected the correct detail but attributed it to the correction, indicating recovery of accurate content without recovery of its original episodic source.

### 3.5 Confidence

Mean confidence was higher for original-detail responses (78.4) than for misinformation responses (69.1), but 17% of misinformation endorsements received confidence ratings of at least 85. High confidence therefore did not guarantee that a retrieved detail originated in the event.

## 4. Discussion

Reliable corrections substantially reduced the influence of contradictory post-event information. Repetition made misinformation more likely to be endorsed, but attributing the correction to a forensic analyst eliminated this repetition effect. The result supports an account in which source reliability changes the relative accessibility or decision weight of competing memory representations.

The source-attribution findings qualify a simple replacement account. Participants sometimes recovered the accurate detail while assigning it to the correction rather than the original event. Accurate content can therefore be available even when episodic provenance remains degraded. This dissociation is consistent with the source-monitoring framework, which distinguishes memory content from judgments about its origin (Johnson et al., 1993).

The results are also compatible with a reconsolidation interpretation. Presentation of the misleading report may have reactivated the event representation, creating an opportunity for later information to be integrated. A reliable correction could then become linked to the reactivated event trace. However, the present behavioral design cannot distinguish integration into a reactivated trace from strategic source weighting at test. These explanations should be treated as compatible alternatives rather than mutually exclusive factual claims.

Repetition may have influenced memory in at least two ways. It increased familiarity of the misleading detail, and it provided multiple opportunities to retrieve or reconstruct that detail. The latter possibility resembles retrieval-induced forgetting, in which selective retrieval impairs related competitors (Anderson et al., 1994). The current design did not directly measure inhibition, so the analogy should not be interpreted as evidence of a shared mechanism.

### 4.1 Limitations

The correction did **not** fully eliminate the effect of repeated misinformation. The interaction was nonsignificant, and misinformation endorsement remained above zero even under high source reliability. The claim of elimination in the Abstract and the opening paragraph of the Discussion is therefore stronger than the inferential evidence supports; “attenuated” is the appropriate description.

The sample consisted of young adults in a controlled laboratory setting. The event was emotionally neutral, corrections followed misinformation within minutes, and the final test occurred after approximately 24 hours. Generalization to consequential autobiographical memories, longer delays, or repeated social exposure requires additional research.

The source manipulation also combined expertise and access. The forensic analyst was described both as professionally trained and as having direct access to the video. Future work should separate these dimensions.

## 5. Author Correction and Post-Lock Data Note

This section has higher version priority than unrevised statements in the preceding manuscript.

1. **Final sample size.** A post-lock identity audit found that two sessions in the nominal N = 186 dataset were duplicate participant records created after browser restarts. Both duplicates occurred in the high-reliability cells. The canonical final sample is therefore **N = 184**, with **46 participants in each of the four cells**. Re-estimation changed no coefficient by more than 0.02 and did not alter any inferential conclusion.

2. **Primary outcome.** Protocol Version 1 named source-attribution accuracy as primary. Preregistered Amendment 1, timestamped before data collection, superseded that designation. The canonical primary outcome is **misinformation endorsement at the delayed test**.

3. **Correction-source labels.** The body text is correct. The **forensic video analyst** is the high-reliability source; the **graduate assistant** is the low-reliability source. The reversed note under Table 2 is a template error.

4. **Handedness.** Left-handedness was a pilot exclusion criterion only. It was not used in the implemented experiment, and no participant was excluded for handedness.

5. **Planned contrast.** The correct value is **p = .018**, not .081.

6. **Effect description.** High source reliability **attenuated** the repetition effect. The data do not justify the stronger claim that the effect was eliminated.

7. **Critical-item count.** There were **eight critical items** and **two noncritical filler items**. Ten test rows do not imply ten critical items.

8. **Retention interval.** The initially planned interval was 48 hours. The implemented interval was approximately 24 hours. Both statements are accurate when their temporal and procedural scope is retained.

9. **Bicycle color.** The event detail was coded as red. Orange-red was accepted as a perceptually equivalent free-recall description. Blue was the contradictory misinformation detail.

10. **Testing rooms.** Room 214 and Room 318 are both correct for different participant cohorts and should not be collapsed into a single conflicting room claim.

---

## 6. Conclusion

Correction of contradictory episodic information depends on more than exposure to an accurate replacement. Source reliability, repetition, and the ability to preserve provenance all contribute to whether outdated information continues to influence recall. The present synthetic results support a cautious conclusion: reliable correction reduced, but did not erase, the influence of repeated misinformation.
